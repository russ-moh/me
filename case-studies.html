The work behind the win.

These are the decisions, recovery moments, and technical artifacts behind the short lines on a résumé. Customer names are intentionally removed; the operational detail is not.

01 / Competitive pursuit

Turning a late entry into the vendor of choice.

A global diagnostics organization was evaluating an oncology order-management program. The field began with more than 15 vendors. By the proof-of-concept stage, three remained. The wrong move would have been a broad product tour. Instead, I co-led an end-to-end solution story around how a provider order becomes coordinated work.

15+Vendors in the initial field
60Stakeholders at the initial experience
$5M+Program for which we became vendor of choice
Decision

Use a tailored hands-on evaluation rather than another custom demo. The buyer needed to feel the workflow in the context of their own users—not hear a feature list.

  • Co-led pursuit strategy from discovery through the onsite POC: narrative, executive framing, and technical experience.
  • Built a provider portal, order workspace, lab scenarios, a lab guide, a microsite, and 38 named test accounts.
  • When the source instance was lost a week before the session, chose a direct rebuild over a risky repair; the rebuilt experience was ready in days.
  • Extended the solution with document-intelligence intake so paper requisitions could enter the same order flow as portal submissions.
Why it mattered

The differentiator was not a feature. It was designing a credible evaluation environment in which users could see the platform operating as one system.

02 / Recovery

When the first answer felt like “overkill,” change the operating model.

A healthcare services team was leaning toward a simpler competitor after an out-of-the-box approach failed to connect. I restarted from the buyer’s actual work: patient interactions, case handling, knowledge, analytics, and mobile teams. The resulting story was deliberately specific enough to be believed.

150Patient records in the tailored environment
400Cases modeled for the workflow
600Interactions used to make it feel real
Move

Replace product-first framing with a working model that showed how a distributed healthcare team could handle intake, knowledge, triage, and follow-through.

  • Reset discovery before rebuilding the demo; avoided optimizing the wrong story.
  • Created a custom environment including a knowledge base, AI-guided analysis, a patient concierge concept, and travel-team communication automation.
  • Used the evidence to give the account team a sharper competitive narrative rather than pretending the first approach was sufficient.
Lesson

A technical solution only feels “too much” when the value architecture is invisible. Make the work visible first.

03 / Solution judgment

The strongest recommendation was to build less.

A regulated manufacturer needed to migrate a corrective-action process. The apparent request was a licensing and customization question. The real decision was whether to stretch generic functionality or use a purpose-built quality capability with the right process model.

8DCorrective-action process at the center
3Viable solution paths evaluated
1Purpose-built recommendation
Judgment

Validated generally available product capability with subject-matter experts before presenting it. Removed roadmap dependence and unnecessary supplier-portal complexity.

  • Separated what was proven, generally available, and future-looking—so the buyer could make a decision based on fact.
  • Advised against a custom build in favor of the product that natively supported the process.
  • Turned a vague migration conversation into a clear, closed-won value story.
Principle

Good solution engineering is not maximizing scope. It is making the hard choice simple enough to act on.

04 / Trust reset

When a sponsor hears “too sales-oriented,” rebuild around the architecture decision.

A payer organization was weighing a custom intake application against a productized healthcare workflow. The sponsor was skeptical and the first conversation did not earn trust. I moved HCLS capability from the opening pitch to the conclusion—starting with the buyer’s data model, routing problem, and scale requirements instead.

2Levels of service clarified in the operating model
1Problem-first onsite technical experience
0Security claims overstated live
Decision

Meet the buyer where the uncertainty actually was: productized HCLS workflow versus custom application—not platform versus competitor.

  • Reset discovery around a free-text intake application with no durable underlying data model and cross-functional demand growing around it.
  • Built a word-for-word talk track, one-page cheat sheet, 20-minute run of show, build-vs-buy battle card, and executive-oriented demo environment.
  • Kept individual names out of the leadership story, used the buyer’s own L1/L2 framing, and documented follow-up commitments instead of overclaiming role-based visibility or encryption live.
  • Created supporting microsites, white papers, business-value assets, and leave-behinds to keep the decision clear after the room.
Outcome

The buyer signed the HCLS order form after the conversation moved from abstract platform debate to a credible operating model.

05 / Field-service proof

Follow the water line—not the feature list.

For a healthcare facilities team, work was disappearing into a black hole and technicians were being dispatched by text. The challenge was not to showcase every field-service capability; it was to demonstrate the exact transition from a request to a resolved work order.

1Hero scenario: a leaking water line
2Personas tested: requester and technician
4Moments in the proof: request, dispatch, route, survey
Judgment

Preserve the operational backbone of the workflow while incorporating stakeholder feedback on the story, portal, notifications, and follow-through.

  • Built a portal shaped around the buyer’s existing experience, a realistic dispatch board, and a live work-order flow from the initial request to technician mobile execution.
  • Modeled the last-mile map and satisfaction survey so decision-makers could see the whole operational loop, not just a polished service desk screen.
  • Ran browser and phone persona testing that surfaced defects server-side validation did not catch.
  • Produced a technical win by aligning the experience with the skeptical operations leader’s actual concern: less invisible work, less drive time, and a clearer handoff.
Principle

Credibility comes from recognizing the buyer’s actual day—not from showing a bigger catalog of features.
